Final information architecture proposal

A help center organized around the work people are trying to finish.

The proposed sidebar uses exactly three navigational layers: section headers, direct articles or expandable topic groups, and shareable article destinations. It covers the current ClassroomIO admin, course, learner, reporting, security, and automation surfaces without copying the dashboard menu.

Corpus-level audit

What the research actually showed

The benchmark was taken from every public article in both support centers—not only their home pages. The counts below come from Teachable’s public sitemap and article pages, Thinkific’s public Zendesk help center API, and ClassroomIO’s current MDX corpus.

Platform
Corpus and structure
What it teaches us
Teachable
194 articles in 9 collections and 38 subcollections. Median article length: 758 words. Average per article: 17.9 links, 5.5 images, and 2.9 callouts. 145 articles contain images; 137 use ordered lists.
It is strongest at deep task grouping, visible breadcrumbs, in-page tables of contents, full-size screenshots, explicit caveats, and related-task linking. The structure separates creator work, student guidance, trust, and support.
Thinkific
1,104 articles in 11 categories and 95 sections. Median article length: 359 words. It has 385 short Quick Reference articles in addition to its main topic tree. Average per article: 14.5 links, 1.2 images, 3.8 H2 headings, and 1.5 ordered lists.
It deliberately supports two search intents: long-form feature guides and short question-shaped answers using “Question / Solution / Learn more.” Its homepage also exposes featured articles, platform status, training, and support escalation.
ClassroomIO now
44 MDX pages across the current Help Center. Median length: 469 words. The corpus averages 7.1 links and 0.8 images per article; 36 of 44 pages contain no images. The explicit sidebar currently places most creator features under one broad “Your Academy” umbrella.
The foundation is sound—static URLs, Orama search, review dates, and a CMS workflow already exist. The main problem is coverage and information architecture: important shipped surfaces are missing, and unrelated concepts such as Community currently sit under Integrations.
The most important benchmark insight: neither competitor treats the product’s visible navigation as the documentation taxonomy. Their categories describe outcomes and user journeys; the product UI path appears inside the article as instructions.
Decisions applied to the sidebar

What to borrow—and what not to copy

Borrow from Teachable

  • Use a small number of durable lifecycle sections.
  • Create subgroups only when a topic has several distinct jobs.
  • Give long guides a local table of contents and anchored headings.
  • Place screenshots directly after the step that creates the pictured state.
  • Use breadcrumbs and related guides to expose the parent context.

Borrow from Thinkific

  • Write titles in the language people search: “How…”, “Why…”, and clear verb-object tasks.
  • Offer short troubleshooting answers alongside deeper feature guides.
  • Make Getting Started a curated journey, not a miscellaneous folder.
  • Expose status, training, and contact routes outside the topic hierarchy.
  • Show prerequisites, availability, and important considerations early.

Avoid their debt

  • Do not duplicate hundreds of quick-reference pages in the visible sidebar.
  • Do not let plan labels or old/new builder variants dominate article titles.
  • Do not depend on screenshots with empty alt text: 21% of Teachable and 56% of Thinkific image tags in the audited corpus lacked useful alt text.
  • Do not expose unfinished features simply because a route exists.

Use ClassroomIO’s strengths

  • Preserve stable, readable URLs and the existing explicit sidebar ordering.
  • Keep Developers and API as separate top navigation destinations.
  • Use existing last_reviewed metadata for maintenance.
  • Keep admin and learner instructions separate when the interface and permissions differ.
Article system

The standard every article should follow

The sidebar only works if each destination solves one recognizable problem. These rules keep articles independent, searchable, linkable, and maintainable.

1 Title the task

Use a verb and object: “Invite students”, “Connect a custom domain”, “Grade a submission”. Avoid internal component names.

2 State the outcome

Open with one sentence explaining who the guide is for and what will be true when they finish.

3 Front-load constraints

Show required role, plan, course type, publication state, and prerequisites before the procedure.

4 Follow the UI path

Write numbered steps with one meaningful action per step. Bold exact labels and include the full navigation path once.

5 Use purposeful visuals

Use tightly cropped, annotated WebP screenshots only where the interface state is hard to infer. Add useful alt text and a short caption.

6 Confirm success

End the procedure with what the user should now see, receive, or be able to do.

7 Link with intent

Use descriptive anchors. Separate “Next step” links from explanatory “Related guides”, and keep each list to a few high-value destinations.

8 Maintain the page

Record review date, verify screenshots after UI changes, preserve redirects, and collect helpfulness and failed-search signals.

Launch boundaries

What is intentionally not in the launch sidebar

Zapier

The app labels this “Coming soon.” Prepare the slug and draft privately, but do not surface it until users can complete the workflow.

Telegram integration

The settings page contains unimplemented profile updates. It should not receive a help article until connection and disconnection work end to end.

Internal operations

Queue administration, jobs internals, contributor setup, and self-hosting operations remain in developer or operator documentation—not customer help.
