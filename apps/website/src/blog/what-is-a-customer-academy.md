A customer academy is a learning site, usually on your own domain, where customers take courses that teach them your product. It holds onboarding paths, feature training and certifications, and it records who finished what.

If you are comparing tools, start with [customer academy software](/customer-education). This guide covers the idea itself: what an academy contains, how it differs from the things it gets confused with, and how to build one.

## Customer academy, help center, LMS: what is the difference?

The three overlap, which is why people mix them up.

A help center answers one question at a time. A customer who knows what they are looking for searches, finds an article, and leaves. Docs work well for that. They do not tell a new customer what to learn first, and they cannot tell you whether anyone understood the article. We wrote a longer comparison in [docs vs company academy](/docs-vs-company-academy).

An LMS is the software underneath. It handles courses, enrollment, exercises, certificates and reports. An academy is what you build with it: a branded, customer-facing site with a catalog and a path through your product.

So an academy usually sits next to your help center, and it is built on an LMS. Most teams that launch one keep their docs and link the two together.

## What goes inside a customer academy

Start with the questions customers ask in their first month. They usually fall into four groups.

1. An onboarding path that takes a new account from signup to the first result they paid for.
2. Feature courses for the parts of the product that are easy to ignore: integrations, reporting, permissions.
3. Role-based training, such as an admin course for the person who sets up the workspace and a separate one for end users.
4. A certification for power users, with an exercise they have to pass and a certificate they can share.

You do not need all four on day one. One onboarding path is enough to launch.

## How to build a customer academy

1. **Pick one audience and one outcome.** New customers who need to reach their first result is a good start. Write the outcome in one sentence before you write a lesson.
2. **Collect what you already have.** Help articles, onboarding calls, webinar recordings and support macros are all raw material. Look at which support questions repeat the most.
3. **Turn it into a sequence.** Order lessons by what the customer needs first, not by the product menu. Keep each lesson short, and end each course with an exercise so you can tell who understood it.
4. **Publish it on your domain.** A branded academy at something like learn.yourcompany.com looks like part of the product. Link to it from onboarding emails, the in-app help menu and your docs.
5. **Look at the data and fix the weakest lesson.** The first useful report is where customers stop. Fix that lesson before you write a new one.

## What to measure

Measure the things the academy is supposed to change. Completion rate shows whether the content works. Time to first result shows whether onboarding is faster. Support tickets on topics the academy covers show whether customers are finding answers there first.

## Common mistakes

- Building a course for every feature before anyone has finished the first one.
- Copying the help center into lessons. A lesson should have an order and an exercise, or it is a doc.
- Hiding the academy behind a login that customers cannot find.
- Never checking where learners stop.
